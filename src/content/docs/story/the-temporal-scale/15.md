**Grundriss:** It was an era of brilliant minds, led by giants like Kandinsky and Paul Klee, driven by ideas that were as passionate as they were bewildering.

**Grundriss:** To many, they were outsiders, too radical, too unorthodox. But at their heart, they were young souls burning with ideals.

**Grundriss:** But the times they were living in turned against what they represented. The world around them changed to one of immense suffering.

**Grundriss:** But despite pressure from the government and the public, they pressed on, expanding the boundaries of expression and art to their fullest.

**Grundriss:** Until the school was closed. That was 1933, I believe. I'm sure you know already.

**Grundriss:** Before it was officially dissolved, Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, the school's final director, gathered the teachers and some students for a meeting. He cracked open a bottle of champagne, and they shared a solemn drink as a toast to their parting.

**Grundriss:** And that's where Bauhaus and my grandfather's artistic journey ended.

**Grundriss:** I grew up listening to this story. If there's one thing I learned from it, I suppose it's to not take anything for granted.

**Grundriss:** Of course, you could say that's just an excuse for why I'm so uptight. Either way, it doesn't really matter.

Her eyes darken, and her voice lowers.

**Grundriss:** I miss them a great deal. Mama, Papa, Hannah, and my dear Grandpa.

**Grundriss:** The days I spent with him were always full of surprises.

**Grundriss:** He'd pop up out of the blue just to say the funniest things: "We'll be covered in mold if we stay curled up in our shells any longer!"

The Story of a Brick

When I was still a student, I spent a year traveling, visiting all the great buildings of the world. I wandered the abandoned amphitheaters of Rome, its crumbling aqueducts and old, neglected churches and temples, sketching in the Mediterranean wind the way Brunelleschi and Donatello did in the 15th century. I was trying to find the essence, the true form of architecture, in those collapsed, unadorned ruins.

Until at last I arrived in India. At their Institute of Management, I met an old man with scars on his face. He told me a story about a brick and an arch.

The architect asks the brick, "Brick, what do you want to be?"

The brick says, "I want to be an arch."

The architect says, "I want you to be an arch too, but arches are too expensive. What if we use a concrete lintel instead?"

Still, the brick tells you only this: "I want to be an arch."

At the end of the story, the old man said, "It's important to respect the material you've chosen. If you're going to use this brick, the best way is to respect it, to let it become what it is, not just to make do with it."
