## Story

**Cornerstone:** "What is architecture?"

**Cornerstone:** I imagine about half the students would answer, "Drawing pretty houses." Unfortunately, I thought the same thing back then.

**Cornerstone:** Students who have read biographies of legendary architects might say that it requires talent, genius, inspiration, and a good head for aesthetics, but those airy words mean very little when it comes to the fundamentals of building a safe, code-compliant modern building.

**Cornerstone:** So, what do you think architecture is?

**"Building":** You're asking me?

**"Building":** I'm just a building.

**Cornerstone:** Fair enough. Let me think about how to put this.

Cornerstone takes a fountain pen from her pocket and begins tapping it against the control console crowded with instruments and knobs.

**Cornerstone:** All buildings are structures created to fulfill a need.

**Cornerstone:** We need places to eat, so we have restaurants. We need places to experience joy, so we build theaters.

**"Building":** Then I am meant to serve people.

**Cornerstone:** Is there something else you'd rather be?

**"Building":** Hmm, I guess I want to know what I am before I decide what I want to be in the future.

**Cornerstone:** The future ...

The architect rolls that much-repeated word around in her mouth.

**Cornerstone:** Can you control the pen in my hand?

**Cornerstone:** Like this. Press it lightly against the paper, then move. Try to draw a straight line.

**Cornerstone:** Looking south toward Barcelona from here, you can see hundreds of different buildings: homes, offices, warehouses, and stores.

**Cornerstone:** Start by drawing whatever you like, any building you can imagine.

**"Building":** I'll try.

The pen in Cornerstone's hand rises upright, then plants its nib against the draft paper.

It wobbles at first, scratching roughly over the draft paper, leaving jagged lines and flecks of ink all over the wrinkled paper.

By the time it accomplishes its first shape, the fountain pen's nib has been bent to an unhealthy angle.

**Cornerstone:** For the record, that pen was expensive. It's a Red Dot Award winner.

**"Building":** I'm terrible at this, aren't I?

**Cornerstone:** Hmm. No, not at all. But you may not have the right tool. Many Awakened use artificial limbs.

**Cornerstone:** But then I can hardly imagine what that would look like for you. I don't know if they make arms big enough for an entire building. We'll just have to make do with what we have.

**"Building":** Professor, will you show me how to use these drawing tools?

**Cornerstone:** Why not?

**Cornerstone:** Today will not be about the trivial intrigues of our work, like how Fallingwater was designed in half a day, nor for that matter, how Parisian city planning organically combined artistry with historical depth ...

**Cornerstone:** And of course, we'd never gossip about the love lives of those famous architects.

**Cornerstone:** To be honest with you all, I'm exhausted mentally and physically. So, what I'd like to do today is encourage you to do the talking.

**Cornerstone:** I want to hear what sort of buildings you hope to design in the future.

**Cornerstone:** To get the ball rolling, I'm going to make an exception and let you in on one of my designs.

**Cornerstone:** When I was a child, I lived in a small city in Poland, nowhere you might have heard of—Nowa Miedź.

**Cornerstone:** During that time, everyone lived in these nearly identical rows of prefab apartment blocks. I'm sure you're all familiar with the concept.

**Cornerstone:** Dismal gray exteriors of concrete, poor soundproofing, and a limited service life.

**Cornerstone:** Not all the time, but most days, I hated them. I think maybe that is where I started on my path to being an architect. I wanted to draw pretty houses that I could imagine living in.

**Student I:** But, Professor, you always say that architecture is not about drawing pretty houses.

**Cornerstone:** I do. But your true passion is often something you find while making mistakes.

**Cornerstone:** What about you, Duvalle?

**Duvalle:** Hmm, I think buildings today are all terribly boring.

Cornerstone raises an eyebrow.

**Duvalle:** Just as Le Corbusier invented Brutalism, I want to invent a new architectural style in the future, something better or at least less boring.

The amiable mood in the classroom fades a little, and the students begin waiting to see how the lecturer will respond to such a pointed remark.

**Cornerstone:** Firstly, I wouldn't call Le Corbusier the "inventor" of Brutalism. Not exactly.

Cornerstone turns back and sketches the basic outline of the Unité d'Habitation on the blackboard.

**Cornerstone:** No visual style, no aesthetic movement, can be the achievement of a single architect.

**Cornerstone:** The blending of military and civilian industrial design languages, the emphasis on practicality, and the use of new building materials are often regarded as the foundations of Brutalism.

**Duvalle:** Like prefab apartment blocks?

**Cornerstone:** Exactly.

**Cornerstone:** Of course, new design languages will appear. I'm only saying they don't get invented out of thin air.

Cornerstone leans against the lectern, takes off her glasses, and gives her hair a shake.

**Cornerstone:** New materials and new needs give rise to new designs. Architecture takes everything in equally—the scarcity born of war and the riches brought forth by prosperity.

**Cornerstone:** These untreated concrete surfaces, left that way because of cost, may not be considered "beautiful" forever.

**Cornerstone:** And Brutalism, as with Googie and Art Nouveau before it, will eventually see its era pass away, becoming an aesthetic that can still be admired but no longer truly understood.

**Duvalle:** So, what will come next?

**Cornerstone:** Actually, as something of a Brutalism devotee, I admit I'm in no rush to meet the future.

**Duvalle:** But it will come all the same, with new designs and new landmarks!

**Duvalle:** I think the next wave will be like the first Macintosh, something like "Computer Cubism."

**Duvalle:** The aviation industry will take the basics of military efficiency into their design language, and the internet will bring maximalism back into view.

As the student talks on and on, a simple smile appears on Cornerstone's face, now without her glasses, as though she were tasting the joy of teaching the next generation for the first time.

**Student II:** Professor, we all want to design buildings that stand out. But if everyone designs something unconventional, doesn't the "unconventional" become ordinary too?

**Cornerstone:** That's an excellent question.

She places the glasses back on her face, takes out the notebook she has carried for years, flips through it, and shows the students a page colored entirely red.

**Cornerstone:** This is a red canvas. If you wanted to add a new color to it, which would you choose?

**Student I:** Ultramarine? Goose yellow? Maybe eggshell white?

**Cornerstone:** Yes, any of those would work, because only when we paint with a different color do the brushstrokes gain meaning.

**Cornerstone:** But the color we choose is built on the foundation of red.

**Cornerstone:** And so-called "unique" architecture doesn't mean the designer's thinking is especially rebellious, advanced, or gifted.

**Cornerstone:** Without the "red" left to us by previous generations, the red we have grown used to, any so-called "uniqueness" or "novelty" loses the very ground it stands on.

**Student II:** I think I understand!

**Student II:** The designs of the world around us influence us in the same way, whether we conform to it or reject it. The old always gives birth to the new.

**Cornerstone:** Precisely that! So, before you truly become architects, I hope you'll learn how everything around you came to be the way it is now.

**Cornerstone:** Some things may seem old-fashioned and boring now, but at one time, they were brand new, revolutionary in their own way, difficult to accept for some, and inspiring for others.

**Cornerstone:** The cycle of new and old will go on forever. You'll mature, and I'll grow old.

**Cornerstone:** ...

**Cornerstone:** Nothing truly lasts forever.

**Cornerstone:** I didn't get to grow old. You didn't grow up either.

**Cornerstone:** Even though we only met by chance.

**"Building":** Professor?

The feel of chalk and blackboard pulls Cornerstone away from her memories of the past.

Is it the past, though? Or the future? Somehow both and neither.

**Cornerstone:** I'm fine. Chalk was the right choice after all. You can grasp the basics already. You've learned to summarize a building's form through geometry.

**Cornerstone:** I'll ask my colleagues to find some materials for you. Next, we'll build a model and test whether this design is feasible.

**Cornerstone:** If its stress structure doesn't collapse in model form, we can tentatively consider the design safe and reasonable.

**Cornerstone:** Of course, it definitely isn't that simple in practice. Otherwise, I wouldn't know such a meticulous supervisor.

**"Building":** Do you think it's a good building? I mean, does it look like me?

**Cornerstone:** You?

Cornerstone begins studying the drawing with newfound energy.

It's a kind of introspection that she really can't begin to fathom. Humans don't have the ability to look inside themselves, to see their own structure and their flaws, not the way this Awakened can.

**Cornerstone:** You've made some significant errors in form and proportion.

**Cornerstone:** But why would you draw yourself? I mean, well, you've already been "built," haven't you?

**"Building":** I'm searching.

**"Building":** I don't know what I'm searching for, maybe something like me.

**"Building":** I don't have feet, and I'm too big for a body. I'll never be able to travel across the sea or go anywhere really. The only thing I can do is build.

The statement exposes an unspoken friction in how the two see each other.

**Cornerstone:** So rather than designing something new, you want to construct more of yourself?

**Cornerstone:** Perhaps the original construction plans are still around. It seems our list of things to find just got longer.

**"Building":** I don't need the construction plans.

**"Building":** I want to "see" myself.

**Cornerstone:** See yourself ...

**Cornerstone:** I've always wondered how you "see" us. But when I remember that we can only speak to you here, where there's a microphone, I think I understand.

**Cornerstone:** You can only "see" the world through these surveillance cameras, right? So for you, this building might as well be the entire world.

**"Building":** Yes.

**Cornerstone:** You've learned things through reading the office computers, but even then all you can see of the world outside are these small screens.

**Cornerstone:** I suppose I must just seem like a blurry figure to you.

**"Building":** I worry that this might be all I'll ever be able to see.

**Cornerstone:** ...

**"Building":** I hope you don't pity me.

**Cornerstone:** Pity? No, not at all. But I've just had an idea!

**Cornerstone:** We're pausing the lesson. It's time for a little emergency construction.

**"Building":** Professor?

**Cornerstone:** Don't worry. My hands-on skills were top of the class at university.

**Cornerstone:** Are you ready? Shall we go and see the world outside?
