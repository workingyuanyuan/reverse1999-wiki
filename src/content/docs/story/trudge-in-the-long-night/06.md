## Story

After 210 left in anger, I heard the shouts of people as the ship set sail.

This debate wasn’t planned; it inevitably disrupted the balance, like dropping a pinch of dust on one side of the scale.

Each pinch wasn't very heavy. Even a breath could blow it away. But bit by bit, they still made their impact.

And the balance began to tilt.

**Atticus:** ...

**Atticus:** ... The retreat is no place for such arguments. From now on, I should not engage in casual conversations.

I am not fond of talking. Silence is a virtue adhered to by many members of the school.

But in prolonged solitude, I learned to converse with myself.

Language, when devoid of other listeners, diminishes in impact but accelerates self-reflection.

**Atticus:** I've finished these books. Later, I'll tell the coordinator about the new book list.

I reach out, pulling out a new book from the bottom of the pile.

A scroll, moved by my sleeve, slips from the gap in the book spine and falls to the floor.

It's a poem I know well, one I used to recite silently during those days of reticence.

**Atticus:** ... "The Golden Verses".

Pythagoras' most famous collection of sayings. Its chronicler is unknown, but the content of the verses is timeless.

**Atticus:** To think deeply, to restrain oneself, to pursue the treasures of the mind and soul.

**Atticus:** To respect the gods and the Supreme, to honor the daemons and inhabitants of the elements, to rise above lower worldly natures.

I first heard this poem from Alma.

At that time, she wasn't yet a 6, and she went by her own name.

And I was young, so young that I needed an elder to read me those lengthy verses.

**Atticus:** Aunt Alma, I don't understand.

**Atticus:** This part here says: "Fate is like a huge millstone on which mortals roll back and forth, enduring endless suffering."

**Atticus:** ... Do we really have to be like this? Is fate such a bad thing?

**Atticus:** Aunt Alma?

**Alma:** Oh ...

Alma moves the book away from her face, yawning under the gazebo.

**Alma:** ... Why are you not asleep yet?

**Alma:** If you ask me, this weather is just perfect for a nap under the grapevines ...

Alma closes her eyes again, ignoring the verses in my hand.

In reality, she is never good with kids.

She takes care of me in place of my mother. For her, it is just another responsibility she has to undertake.

And my mother has more important matters—the responsibilities of a 6.

One day, that responsibility will also belong to me.

**Alma:** Atticus, why don't you come over here and take a nap?

**Alma:** Thinking about fate isn't something a child your age should be preoccupied with.

**Atticus:** But, I want to know—

I am eager to ask my question, but Alma just shakes her head.

**Alma:** Oh, yes, you're a good child. You've heard much talk from people about 6, fate, truth, the "revelation," and other such grand topics.

**Alma:** But if you stay fixated on these abstract concepts, the happiness that’s right in front of you will slip away.

Alma takes the verses out of my hands and pats me on the back.

**Alma:** Come, Atticus, lie down here. It's a beautiful day, and these grapes are just ripe.

**Alma:** I know you're still thinking about the exhortations in "The Golden Verses"—

**Alma:** "Very few people know how to escape their own misfortune, for they have not yet seen their own fate."

**Alma:** But this sunlight, the sound of the wind, and these naps are just as much a part of who we are.

**Alma:** Let's lie here together, savoring these rare, precious moments.

Alma places "The Golden Verses" on her face and drifts back to sleep.

She has always been a humorous and light-hearted person, standing out like a glass bead among pebbles on this island of strict rules.

And I am a solitary and precocious child, prematurely subjected to the burdensome weight of responsibility.

Only around Alma did I feel any relief.

**Atticus:** ... Lie down, huh?

I hear the birds singing, the wind whispering, the distant sound of waves crashing against the shore.

I have read many profound books, memorized many ancient maxims, and dealt with many complex calculations.

But it was Alma that provided me with "The Golden Verses" with a page filled with bright annotations.

It is a perfect, comfortable warmth, a moment of tranquility that brings no anxiety.

Like the waves that occasionally lap my toes at the edge of the island, gently approaching and then retreating, providing a cool sensation that brings joy to a stroll along the beach.

—She was like that.

So, I leave my sheltered home, tread on the soft sand, and experience the understanding she spoke of.

Experienced what others call "playtime."

The sand is moist, the sea breeze is salty, my clothes feel heavy, and my skin is clammy with droplets of moisture.

The vast sea always by my side, with waves lapping against the shore, yet they never soaked me fully.

Looking back now, my innate sensitivity as a child picked up on those anomalies long before my own wisdom did.

**Atticus:** ...

Suddenly feeling fear, I stop asking questions, merely holding my breath and cautiously shifting my feet forward.

Aunt Alma's back is close, and the moonlight casts a gentle glow on her fine, blonde hair.

**Atticus:** Aunt Alma?

**Alma:** Hmm? Ah ... look at that, I didn’t even notice you were back ...

Alma's voice is soft, her hands warm ... turning hotter. She wraps her arms around my shoulders, drawing my stiff body into her embrace.

The sea rises, climbing up to my shins.

**Alma:** I was sorting my thoughts. Sorry, I didn't hear you at all.

**Alma:** Until just now, my mind was a mess, but I’m thinking more clearly now. I've figured it out.

**Alma:** Don't be afraid, child.

**Alma:** "Pray to the gods for what you are about to do, and pray that all goes well."

Alma pulls the man's hand from my arm.

Her voice is calm, her face sorrowful. She turns her head slightly and hugs me tighter.

**Alma:** Just, oh, Atticus, my poor child.

**Alma:** My poor child ...

Alma became 6 that day.

**Atticus:** Let go of me!

My fear originates from it, yet when I feel uncertain, I still return here, like a child who can't imagine straying too far from home.

**Atticus:** ...

**Atticus:** "Pray to the gods for what you are about to do" ...

Complete, wise, her words clear and impeccable like the ancient maxims.

**Alma:** "Pray to the gods for what you are about to do, and pray that all goes well."

**Alma:** "Once familiar with this practice, you will understand the constitution of both eternal gods and mortal men."

**Alma:** "You will know the extent of all things, the boundaries of their entirety, and what connects them together."

**Alma:** "You will see, as you should, that the nature of the universe flows in all things alike."

**Alma:** "Thus you will not hope for what is beyond your reach, and you will not be deceived."

**Atticus:** ... "Revelation" ...

**Atticus:** Is this the "revelation" that falls upon a 6?

And this is "the blessing from above."

For a long time afterward, I could not sleep in peace. I would often sneak down to the beach, listening to the waves until exhaustion at last closed my eyes.

In a daze, a voice of wisdom would say—

"Don't fear, child. ' Remember that the grace of the gods is given in the form of disaster, and endure it patiently. Such is fate; do not complain.'"

**Atticus:** ...

I vaguely realize something, and I open my eyes to see the silent boulder high on the mountain overlooking the island.

One day it will crush me, just as it has crushed Alma.

So, I cover my ears and bend down.

From then on, I no longer gaze at the boulder on the mountain nor look at the teetering bottle on the table.

... Until much later, when I have a brief conversation with a girl named Sophia.
