## Cover

An arcanist's work. First exhibited in the 1990s for 28 years. Completed in winter, on February 9. Originally exhibited in Nowa Miedź, Republic of Poland, later touring through several European countries. Now on display at the St. Pavlov Foundation.

Dimensions: 67 3/4×31 1/8×16 1/8 in.

Medium: Concrete

Fragrance Notes: Woody - Cedar, Burnt Coffee, Charcoal, Fragrant Minerals, Dandelion

Afflatus: Gray Walls of the Old Building \[Mineral\] Architectural Design

## Item

### "Lightweight"

A pair of polygonal glasses with a thin, spotless titanium frame. According to the interviewee, her mentor gave them to her with these words: "Architecture is heavy—but thought should be light." Nowhere does its virtue suit better than the bridge of the interviewee's nose.

### "Froebel's Gifts"

A set of concrete building blocks modeled after children's educational toys, made up of basic geometric shapes—circles, triangles, rectangles, and others.

For the interviewee, even though the blocks carry arcane functions like decomposition, construction, and material absorption, she still prefers to think of them as "toys," a reminder to pay attention to architecture's foundation—the quiet rhythm hidden between each mass and form.

### "Home on the Move"

A small drafting case stamped with the Foundation symbol, used to hold the architect's drawing board, a coffee-stained sketchbook, and countless "souvenirs" picked up from who-knows-where. But its most important function is simple and plain: a scarf printed with patterns from her hometown, tied to the handle so the interviewee can carry it with her wherever she goes.

## Story

### Flower Pots or Gray Walls

Ceramic, red brick, glass, straw ... people kept chasing whatever new building style came along, just like the three little pigs trying to find the house that actually stood up against the howling wind. But the answer the world finally settled on was a flower pot from 1867.

Soon came engineering's quiet helpers. Building after building of durable, cheap, quickly assembled precast concrete cages became the best solution for housing people. Meanwhile, flower pots grew ever more refined—those expensive, hand-glazed ceramic pieces would never again be coated in that coarse gray. From then on, the gray belonged only to human dwellings.

Years after one architect's masterwork was completed in Marseille, people finally discovered the beauty unique to that gray. He stripped away the plaster and paint and revealed the material's true face to the world. At the end of this cement fairy tale, the architect gave it an honest name: Brutalism.

One woman was born into that gray and, as an architect, gave the rest of her life to it—which is why we can still find it standing beside still waters, a monument bearing the weight of the Industrial Age.

### No Architects Needed

Pandora Wilson: It's rare to find an interviewee like you—someone with a stable job and a successful career.

Cornerstone: I'm used to the interview process—the pitch-perfect smile, the squared shoulders, the expensive suit ... But I often find myself asking whether the world even needs architects.

Cornerstone: Oh, don't take that as self-deprecation.

Pandora Wilson: Would you care to elaborate on what you mean by the world not needing architects"?

Cornerstone: Of course.

Cornerstone: Architects can't make concrete any cheaper or more durable. We didn't invent rebar, and we certainly didn't create safety glass. That's all thanks to technological innovation.

Cornerstone: It's a meaningful question. Did say, Brutalism, really come about as a work of conscious design, or was it just the inevitable outcome of affordable concrete as a material?

Cornerstone: All we can do is take what the era hands us and shape its rhythm into space and form.

Pandora Wilson: A very thought-provoking philosophy.

Cornerstone: I knew you'd like that one. Clients and business magazines always eat that one up.

Cornerstone: Oh—you're cutting that last part, right?

### The Cabinet of Vivification

> "And the Architect injected into us ... a drop of soul, you say?"

Every building carries a measure of life within it, no matter how big or small.

This castle is no exception, though its residents are rather unusual. Each night, when the clock strikes midnight, bedsheets rise from their chambers and float through the halls, lit only by the torches' ghostly green flames.

Deep within the castle, however, a solitary silhouette is too immersed in her work to pay it any mind. Testing the strength of "tendons," calculating the dimensions of "bones," and adjusting the proportions of "muscles" ... She refines every detail with the same deliberation with which time has worn the bricks of the castle.

Bricks, yes, bricks—once shaped by life, now shaping another. This is no mere flight of fancy. Stories, memories, and habits linger still in the nooks and crannies, can be touched by physical hands, can be told by wandering spirits.

"The castle itself isn't enough!" the architect declares. "Spirits deserve a place warmer than coffins or tombs to stay in, and awakened bricks need somewhere to store their stories." Before fleeting time devours all, she resolves to build a residence where every life may nestle.

#### Wisdom Made Manifest

A soft, bouncy ... brain made of cloth. The author later confirmed that there is no living tissue woven into this delicate hat.

Every mad scientist takes pride in their brain—the cornerstone of all their achievements and the source of their superiority when proclaiming their manic manifestos. It was for this very reason that the interviewee commissioned this hat before October's arrival.

#### Pulse Visualized

A scarlet ribbon brooch inspired by the interviewee's own doodle.

Imbuing the lifeless with life, emotion, and a beating heart is precisely what a mad scientist would spend their October doing. And thus, for this month only, her studio shall become a dark workshop for the vivification of boulders.

The interviewee introduces the festivities while clad in a special costume. She encourages our readers to come and see for themselves.

#### "The Next Step"

Elegant and balanced with a satisfying heft, it has every quality of an ideal staff. It directs the flow of stones by day and summons lightning by the dark of night. More importantly, it might just make negotiations with the client go more smoothly.

"Why would a mad scientist have clients?"

"Does it matter? Don't tell me you've never wished for a cane like this on a difficult workday," answers the interviewee.
